summary: Modal verbs like *können* (can) and *müssen* (must) say what someone can, has to, wants to or may do. In the present tense, *ich* and *er / sie / es* have the same form with no ending, *ich kann* and *er kann*. The second verb goes to the end as an infinitive.

## Modal verb + infinitive

A modal verb says how someone relates to an action: they can do it, have to, want to or are allowed to. The action itself is a second verb in the infinitive, the basic form like <De>fahren</De> or <De>arbeiten</De>. As the word order topic shows, the modal verb is in position 2, or first in a yes/no question, and the infinitive goes to the very end.

## The present tense forms

Modal verbs do not follow the normal present tense endings. Here is <De>können</De> (can):

Three things to notice:

- <De>ich</De> and <De>er / sie / es</De> have the same form, and it has no ending: <De>ich kann</De>, <De>er kann</De>. Never add <De>-t</De> here.
- In the singular, most modal verbs have a different vowel from the infinitive: <De>können</De> → <De>ich kann</De>.
- The plural is regular. <De>wir</De> and <De>sie / Sie</De> use the infinitive, and <De>ihr</De> takes <De>-t</De>.

The other modal verbs work the same way. Here are the forms you have to learn; bold forms have a different vowel. <De>sollen</De> keeps its vowel everywhere. <De>du musst</De> adds only <De>-t</De>, because the stem already ends in an s sound.

<RuleTable wide>

| infinitive | <De plain>ich</De> | <De plain>du</De> | <De plain>er / sie / es</De> | <De plain>ihr</De> |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <De plain>müssen</De> | <De plain>**muss**</De> | <De plain>**musst**</De> | <De plain>**muss**</De> | <De plain>müsst</De> |
| <De plain>dürfen</De> | <De plain>**darf**</De> | <De plain>**darfst**</De> | <De plain>**darf**</De> | <De plain>dürft</De> |
| <De plain>wollen</De> | <De plain>**will**</De> | <De plain>**willst**</De> | <De plain>**will**</De> | <De plain>wollt</De> |

## <De>können</De>, <De>müssen</De>, <De>dürfen</De>

**1. <De>können</De>: can.** Someone is able to do something, or something is possible.

## <De>nicht müssen</De> or <De>nicht dürfen</De>?

English "must not" means something is forbidden. German <De>nicht müssen</De> usually does not mean that. It means that something is not necessary.

- <De>du musst nicht</De>: you don't have to
- <De>du darfst nicht</De>: you must not, you're not allowed to

## <De>wollen</De> and <De>sollen</De>

**1. <De>wollen</De>: want to.** Your own wish or plan. Careful: <De>ich will</De> means "I want", not "I will". When you ask for something in a shop or a café, use the polite <De>möchten</De> instead.

**2. <De>sollen</De>: be supposed to.** Someone else wants it: a doctor, a boss, your parents. With <De>Soll ich …?</De> you ask what the other person wants you to do.

## <De>möchten</De> and <De>mögen</De>

**<De>möchten</De>: would like.** This is the polite way to say what you want, in a shop, a café or at the doctor's. It can also go with a noun alone, without a second verb. Its endings are almost the normal ones, but <De>er / sie / es möchte</De> has no <De>-t</De>: it is the same as <De>ich möchte</De>.

<RuleTable equal>

| singular | plural |
| --- | --- |
| <De plain>ich möcht**e**</De> | <De plain>wir möcht**en**</De> |
| <De plain>du möcht**est**</De> | <De plain>ihr möcht**et**</De> |
| <De plain>er / sie / es möcht**e**</De> | <De plain>sie / Sie möcht**en**</De> |

</RuleTable>
