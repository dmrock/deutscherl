---
title: 'Negation: nicht and kein'
summary: German has two words for "not". *kein* goes with a noun that would have *ein* or no article, as in *kein Auto*. Everything else takes *nicht*. To say "yes" to a negative question, use *doch*.
---

## <De>kein</De>: no, not a, not any

<De>kein</De> negates a noun. Use it where the positive sentence has <De>ein</De> or no article at all: <De>ein Auto</De> → <De>kein Auto</De>, <De>Zeit</De> → <De>keine Zeit</De>. It has the same endings as <De>ein</De>, and it also has a plural form, <De>keine</De>.

<RuleTable wide>

| masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <De plain>ein → **kein**</De> | <De plain>eine → **keine**</De> | <De plain>ein → **kein**</De> | <De plain>– → **keine**</De> |

</RuleTable>

## <De>nicht</De>: not

<De>nicht</De> negates everything else: a verb, an adjective, a place, or a noun with <De>der</De>, <De>die</De>, <De>das</De> or <De>mein</De>. Its position depends on what you negate.

**1. The whole sentence: at the end.** <De>nicht</De> comes after the verb, after objects like <De>den Mann</De>, and after time words like <De>heute</De>.

## <De>kein</De> or <De>nicht</De>?

With nouns, the article decides.

<RuleTable equal>

| <De>kein</De> | <De>nicht</De> |
| --- | --- |
| noun with <De>ein</De> | noun with <De>der</De>, <De>die</De>, <De>das</De> |
| noun without an article | noun with <De>mein</De>, <De>dein</De> … |

</RuleTable>

## Answering: <De>doch</De> and <De>nein</De>

To answer a negative question with "yes", say <De>doch</De>, not <De>ja</De>. <De>nein</De> agrees with the negative question.
