title: 'Articles and gender: der, die, das'
summary: Every German noun has a gender, masculine (*der*), feminine (*die*) or neuter (*das*), and in the plural every noun takes *die*. The word for "a" is *ein* or *eine*. Learn each noun together with its article.
---

## Three genders

Every German noun has a grammatical gender: masculine, feminine or neuter. You see it in the article, the little word before the noun: <De>der</De>, <De>die</De> or <De>das</De>, all meaning "the". Nouns are always written with a capital letter.

**1. People.** For people, the gender usually matches: a man is <De>der</De>, a woman is <De>die</De>. But <De>das Kind</De> (the child) and <De>das Mädchen</De> (the girl) are neuter.

**2. Things.** For things, the gender usually has nothing to do with the meaning. A table is masculine, a lamp is feminine, and a book is neuter. In the plural, every noun takes <De>die</De>.

So always learn a noun with its article: not just <De>Tisch</De>, but <De>der Tisch</De>. Learn the plural with it too, because the plural forms differ: <De>der Tisch, die Tische</De>; <De>die Lampe, die Lampen</De>; <De>das Buch, die Bücher</De>.

## <De>ein</De> or <De>der</De>?

<De>ein</De> and <De>eine</De> mean "a". Use them when you mention something for the first time, or when it is one of many. Use <De>der</De>, <De>die</De>, <De>das</De> when it is clear which one you mean: you have mentioned it before, or there is only one, like the sun.

The plural has no word for "a": you use the noun on its own.

## Clues for the gender

Most nouns give no clue, but some endings and groups do.

**1. Usually <De>die</De>.** Nouns ending in <De>-ung</De>, <De>-heit</De>, <De>-keit</De> and <De>-ion</De> are almost always feminine: <De>die Zeitung, die Gesundheit, die Information</De>. So are women's jobs with <De>-in</De>: <De>die Lehrerin</De>. Many nouns ending in <De>-e</De> are feminine too: <De>die Lampe, die Tasche</De>.

**2. Always <De>das</De>.** The ending <De>-chen</De> makes a word small, and it always gives <De>das</De>: <De>das Brot</De> → <De>das Brötchen</De> (the roll), <De>die Maus</De> → <De>das Mäuschen</De>. <De>das Mädchen</De> is one of these words too. Verbs used as nouns are neuter as well: <De>essen</De> → <De>das Essen</De> (the food, the meal).

**3. Usually <De>der</De>.** Days, months and seasons are almost always masculine: <De>der Montag, der Mai, der Sommer</De>. So are most nouns for men and for people who do something, ending in <De>-er</De>: <De>der Lehrer, der Fahrer</De> (the driver).

**4. Compound nouns.** German often joins words into one long noun. The last part decides the gender: <De>das Haus</De> + <De>die Tür</De> → <De>die Haustür</De> (the front door), <De>kühl</De> (cool) + <De>der Schrank</De> (the cupboard) → <De>der Kühlschrank</De> (the fridge).

<AustrianNote id="at1">
  A few nouns have a different gender in Austria. <De>E-Mail</De> is often neuter there (<De>das E-Mail</De>), while in Germany it is feminine (<De>die E-Mail</De>). Cola is the same: Austrians say <De>das Cola</De>, most Germans <De>die Cola</De>. Both are correct standard German.
</AustrianNote>
