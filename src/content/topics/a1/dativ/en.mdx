title: 'Dativ: to whom, with whom'
summary: The dative (*Dativ*) answers the question *Wem?* (to whom?). It comes after *mit, bei, zu, nach, von, aus*, after verbs like *helfen*, and for the person who gets something. The articles become *dem, der, dem*, and in the plural *den*.
---

## What is the dative?

The article of a German noun changes with the noun's role in the sentence. These forms are called cases. You already know the nominative (the subject) and the accusative (the direct object, <De>Wen?</De> or <De>Was?</De>).

The dative is another case. It often marks the person who gets something, or the person something is done for. To find it, ask <De>Wem?</De> (to whom? whom?).

You use the dative in three situations:

1. after the prepositions <De>mit, bei, zu, nach, von, aus</De>,
2. after some verbs, like <De>helfen</De> (to help),
3. for the person who gets something, with verbs like <De>geben</De> (to give).

## Dative articles

<RuleTable>

| article | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <De plain>der · die · das</De> | <De plain>**dem**</De> | <De plain>**der**</De> | <De plain>**dem**</De> | <De plain>**den** …**n**</De> |
| <De plain>ein · eine</De> | <De plain>ein**em**</De> | <De plain>ein**er**</De> | <De plain>ein**em**</De> | – |
| <De plain>kein · keine</De> | <De plain>kein**em**</De> | <De plain>kein**er**</De> | <De plain>kein**em**</De> | <De plain>kein**en** …**n**</De> |
| <De plain>mein · meine</De> | <De plain>mein**em**</De> | <De plain>mein**er**</De> | <De plain>mein**em**</De> | <De plain>mein**en** …**n**</De> |

</RuleTable>

Masculine and neuter look the same: they end in **-em**. Feminine ends in **-er**. The plural ends in **-en**, and the noun gets an extra **-n**. <De>ein</De> has no plural. All possessive articles (<De>mein, dein, sein, ihr, unser</De> …) work like <De>kein</De>: <De>deinem, ihrer, unseren</De>.

**The extra -n in the plural.** In the dative plural, the noun itself ends in -n: <De>die Kinder</De> → <De>den Kindern</De>, <De>die Freunde</De> → <De>den Freunden</De>. There is no extra -n when the plural already ends in -n (<De>die Eltern, die Frauen</De>) or in -s (<De>die Babys, die Autos</De>).

## Prepositions with the dative

These six prepositions are always followed by the dative:

- <De>mit</De>: with. Also for transport: <De>mit dem Bus, mit der U-Bahn</De>. But on foot is <De>zu Fuß</De>.
- <De>bei</De>: at someone's home, at a shop or at the doctor's: <De>bei meiner Oma</De>.
- <De>zu</De>: to a person or a place: <De>zu meinem Freund</De>.
- <De>nach</De>: after (time). With cities and countries, <De>nach</De> also means "to": <De>nach Wien</De>. There is no article there, so you see no dative ending.
- <De>von</De>: from, of: <De>von meiner Tante</De>.
- <De>aus</De>: out of, and where someone comes from: <De>aus der Schweiz</De>.

Two fixed phrases: <De>zu Hause</De> means "at home", <De>nach Hause</De> means "(going) home".

## Short forms: <De>zum, zur, beim, vom</De>

Some prepositions join with <De>dem</De> or <De>der</De> into one word. In everyday German, these short forms are the normal ones.

<RuleTable>

| preposition + article | short form |
| --- | --- |
| <De plain>zu dem</De> | <De plain>**zum**</De> |
| <De plain>zu der</De> | <De plain>**zur**</De> |
| <De plain>bei dem</De> | <De plain>**beim**</De> |
| <De plain>von dem</De> | <De plain>**vom**</De> |

</RuleTable>

With <De>bei</De> and <De>von</De>, the feminine has no short form: <De>bei der, von der</De>.

## Verbs with the dative

Some verbs take a dative object, not an accusative one. The most common:

**1. Helping, thanking, congratulating.** The person you help (<De>helfen</De>), thank (<De>danken</De>) or congratulate (<De>gratulieren</De>) is in the dative.

**2. Liking, belonging, tasting.** With <De>gefallen, gehören, schmecken</De>, the thing is the subject, and the person is in the dative. <De>gefallen</De> means "to be liked by": word for word, the thing "pleases" the person. <De>gehören</De> means "to belong to", and <De>schmecken</De> means "to taste good to".

## Giving someone something

Verbs like <De>geben</De> (to give), <De>schenken</De> (to give as a present) and <De>zeigen</De> (to show) have two objects. The person who gets something is in the dative. The thing is in the accusative. When both are nouns, the person usually comes first.
