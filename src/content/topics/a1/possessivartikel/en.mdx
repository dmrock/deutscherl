---
title: 'Possessivartikel: my, your, his, her'
summary: Possessive articles (*Possessivartikel*) say who something belongs to, like *mein* (my), *dein* (your), *sein* (his) and *ihr* (her). The owner decides which word you use. The noun after it decides the ending, which works like *ein*, as in *mein Bruder* and *meine Schwester*.
---

## Who does it belong to?

Possessive articles are words like "my" and "your". They stand before a noun, in the place of <De>der</De> or <De>ein</De>, and say who the person or thing belongs to: <De>ein Bruder</De> (a brother), <De>mein Bruder</De> (my brother). Each personal pronoun has its own possessive article.

<RuleTable equal>

| singular | plural |
| --- | --- |
| <De plain>ich → **mein**</De> | <De plain>wir → **unser**</De> |
| <De plain>du → **dein**</De> | <De plain>ihr → **euer**</De> |
| <De plain>er, es → **sein**</De> | <De plain>sie → **ihr**</De> |
| <De plain>sie → **ihr**</De> | <De plain>Sie → **Ihr**</De> |

</RuleTable>

Watch out for <De>ihr</De>. As a possessive article, <De>ihr</De> means "her" or "their". When you talk to several people you call <De>ihr</De> (you), "your" is <De>euer</De>. With a capital letter, <De>Ihr</De> is the polite "your" that goes with <De>Sie</De>.

## <De>sein</De> or <De>ihr</De>?

The owner decides which word you use:

- <De>sein</De> when the owner is masculine or neuter (<De>er</De>, <De>es</De>): Paul, <De>das Kind</De>, <De>das Hotel</De>.
- <De>ihr</De> when the owner is feminine (<De>sie</De>) or when there are several owners (<De>sie</De>, plural): Anna, Tom and Lisa.

The noun after it does not change this choice. It only decides the ending. Paul's sister is <De>seine Schwester</De>, and Anna's brother is <De>ihr Bruder</De>.

## Endings like <De>ein</De>

Possessive articles take the same endings as <De>ein</De>. The table shows the nominative (the subject) and, after the arrow, the accusative (the direct object).

<RuleTable wide>

| masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <De plain>ein → **einen**</De> | <De plain>eine → eine</De> | <De plain>ein → ein</De> | – |
| <De plain>mein → **meinen**</De> | <De plain>meine → meine</De> | <De plain>mein → mein</De> | <De plain>meine → meine</De> |
| <De plain>unser → **unseren**</De> | <De plain>unsere → unsere</De> | <De plain>unser → unser</De> | <De plain>unsere → unsere</De> |
| <De plain>euer → **euren**</De> | <De plain>eure → eure</De> | <De plain>euer → euer</De> | <De plain>eure → eure</De> |

</RuleTable>

In the nominative, masculine and neuter have no ending: <De>mein Vater</De>, <De>mein Kind</De>. Feminine and plural add <De>-e</De>: <De>meine Mutter</De>, <De>meine Eltern</De>. In the accusative, only the masculine changes: <De>meinen Vater</De>. <De>ein</De> has no plural, but possessive articles do: <De>meine Eltern</De>.

<De>euer</De> gets shorter when it takes an ending: <De>eure</De>, <De>euren</De>. The long forms, like <De>euere</De>, are correct too, but this page uses the short ones.

<AustrianNote id="at1">
  In Austria, Cola is neuter (<De>das Cola</De>), as in southern Germany and Switzerland. The possessive article shows the gender, so Austrians say <De>mein Cola</De>. In the rest of Germany you will also hear the feminine <De>die Cola</De>, and with it <De>meine Cola</De>. Both genders are correct.
</AustrianNote>

## In the dative

In the dative, possessive articles take the same endings as <De>ein</De> and <De>kein</De>: <De>-em</De> for masculine and neuter, <De>-er</De> for feminine and <De>-en</De> for the plural. In the dative plural, the noun gets an extra <De>-n</De> (<De>meinen Kindern</De>). There is no extra <De>-n</De> when the plural already ends in <De>-n</De> (<De>meinen Eltern</De>) or in <De>-s</De> (<De>meinen Handys</De>).

<RuleTable wide>

| masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <De plain>mein → **meinem**</De> | <De plain>meine → **meiner**</De> | <De plain>mein → **meinem**</De> | <De plain>meine → **meinen** …**n**</De> |

</RuleTable>

You need the dative after <De>mit</De>, <De>bei</De> and <De>zu</De>, and with verbs like <De>helfen</De> (to help) and <De>gehören</De> (to belong to).
