---
title: 'Present tense: regular verbs'
summary: Most German verbs are regular in the present tense (*Präsens*). Take the stem, the infinitive without *-en*, and add the ending for the person. The present tense is also used for the future.
---

## Infinitive and stem

The infinitive is the basic form of a verb, the one you find in a dictionary. It almost always ends in <De>-en</De>: <De>wohnen</De> (to live), <De>machen</De> (to do, to make), <De>lernen</De> (to learn).

Take away <De>-en</De> and you get the stem: <De>wohn-</De>, <De>mach-</De>, <De>lern-</De>. In the present tense, the stem gets an ending, and the ending shows who does the action.

Two pairs look the same. The forms for <De>wir</De> and <De>sie / Sie</De> are the infinitive. The forms for <De>er / sie / es</De> and <De>ihr</De> both end in <De>-t</De>. The pronoun tells you who it is.

## Stems ending in <De>-t</De> or <De>-d</De>

When the stem ends in <De>-t</De> or <De>-d</De>, as in <De>arbeiten</De> (to work) or <De>finden</De> (to find), an extra <De>-e-</De> comes before <De>-st</De> and <De>-t</De>. Without it, you could not hear the ending.

<RuleTable equal>

| singular | plural |
| --- | --- |
| <De plain>ich arbeit**e**</De> | <De plain>wir arbeit**en**</De> |
| <De plain>du arbeit**est**</De> | <De plain>ihr arbeit**et**</De> |
| <De plain>er / sie / es arbeit**et**</De> | <De plain>sie / Sie arbeit**en**</De> |

</RuleTable>

## Stems ending in <De>-s</De>, <De>-ß</De> or <De>-z</De>

When the stem already ends in an s sound, as in <De>heißen</De> (to be called), <De>tanzen</De> (to dance) or <De>reisen</De> (to travel), the <De>du</De> form adds only <De>-t</De>: <De>du heißt</De>, <De>du tanzt</De>, <De>du reist</De>. So it looks like the <De>er / sie / es</De> form.

## When to use the present tense

German has only one present tense. There is no separate form for "I am doing": <De>ich koche</De> means both "I cook" and "I'm cooking". You use it:

**1. For now and for habits.** What is happening right now, and what happens often or always.

**2. For the future.** When a word like <De>morgen</De> (tomorrow) or <De>nächste Woche</De> (next week) shows the time, the present tense is the normal way to talk about the future.

**Not all verbs are regular.** Some common verbs change their vowel with <De>du</De> and <De>er / sie / es</De>: <De>fahren</De> → <De>du fährst</De>, <De>sprechen</De> → <De>du sprichst</De>. The endings stay the same. <De>sein</De> and <De>haben</De> are irregular too and have their own topic.

<AustrianNote id="at1">
  <De>raunzen</De> is a typical Austrian word: it means to moan and complain all the time, often about small things. It is also used in Bavaria. Its stem ends in <De>-z</De>, so the <De>du</De> form is <De>du raunzt</De>, like <De>du tanzt</De>.
</AustrianNote>
