---
title: 'Personal pronouns, sein and haben'
summary: Personal pronouns (*ich, du, er, sie, es* …) stand for people and things. The two most important verbs, *sein* (to be) and *haben* (to have), are irregular, so learn their forms by heart.
---

## Personal pronouns

Personal pronouns are short words like "I", "you" and "she". They stand for a person or a thing, so you do not have to repeat its name.

<RuleTable>

| pronoun | meaning |
| --- | --- |
| <De>ich</De> | I |
| <De>du</De> | you (one person) |
| <De>er</De> | he |
| <De>sie</De> | she |
| <De>es</De> | it |
| <De>wir</De> | we |
| <De>ihr</De> | you (several people) |
| <De>sie</De> | they |
| <De>Sie</De> | you (polite) |

</RuleTable>

Unlike English "I", <De>ich</De> is written with a small letter, except at the start of a sentence. <De>Sie</De> (polite "you") always has a capital S.

## <De>du</De>, <De>ihr</De> or <De>Sie</De>?

German has three words for "you".

**1. <De>du</De> and <De>ihr</De>.** <De>du</De> is for one person you know well: friends, family, children, and often colleagues. <De>ihr</De> is the plural of <De>du</De>: two or more such people.

## <De>er</De>, <De>sie</De>, <De>es</De> for things

Every German noun has a gender: masculine (<De>der</De>), feminine (<De>die</De>) or neuter (<De>das</De>). The pronoun follows this gender, also for things. So a key (<De>der Schlüssel</De>) is <De>er</De>, not "it". In the plural, everything is <De>sie</De>.

<RuleTable wide>

| masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| <De plain>der → **er**</De> | <De plain>die → **sie**</De> | <De plain>das → **es**</De> | <De plain>die → **sie**</De> |

</RuleTable>

## <De>sein</De>: to be

<De>sein</De> is irregular: its forms do not follow the normal endings, and each one must be learned.

<RuleTable equal>

| singular | plural |
| --- | --- |
| <De plain>ich **bin**</De> | <De plain>wir **sind**</De> |
| <De plain>du **bist**</De> | <De plain>ihr **seid**</De> |
| <De plain>er / sie / es **ist**</De> | <De plain>sie / Sie **sind**</De> |

</RuleTable>

With <De>sein</De> you say who someone is, where they are from, and what someone or something is like. A job on its own has no article: <De>Lehrerin</De>, not <De>eine Lehrerin</De> (see the <De>ich</De> example above). With an adjective, the article is back: <De>eine gute Lehrerin</De> (a good teacher). Your age also goes with <De>sein</De>, as in English.

## <De>haben</De>: to have

<De>haben</De> is irregular only with <De>du</De> and <De>er / sie / es</De>: the b disappears.

<RuleTable equal>

| singular | plural |
| --- | --- |
| <De plain>ich habe</De> | <De plain>wir haben</De> |
| <De plain>du **hast**</De> | <De plain>ihr habt</De> |
| <De plain>er / sie / es **hat**</De> | <De plain>sie / Sie haben</De> |

</RuleTable>

In a yes/no question, the verb comes first: <De>Hast du …?</De>, <De>Sind Sie …?</De>

**<De>haben</De> where English says "be".** Some feelings and states go with <De>haben</De> and a noun: <De>Hunger haben</De> (to be hungry), <De>Durst haben</De> (to be thirsty), <De>Angst haben</De> (to be afraid), <De>Zeit haben</De> (to be free, to have time), <De>Geburtstag haben</De> (to have your birthday).

<AustrianNote id="at1">
  In Austria, the greeting often shows whether you say <De>du</De> or <De>Sie</De>. <De>Servus</De> is a friendly "hello" and "bye" for people you call <De>du</De>. With <De>Sie</De>, people say <De>Grüß Gott</De> (hello) and <De>Auf Wiedersehen</De> (goodbye). Both greetings are common in Bavaria too, but in most of Germany people say <De>Hallo</De> and <De>Guten Tag</De>.
</AustrianNote>
