---
title: 'Akkusativ: the direct object'
summary: The accusative is the case of the direct object, the person or thing the action is done to. Only masculine words change. *der* becomes *den*, and *ein* becomes *einen*.
---

## What is the accusative?

In German, the article of a noun changes with the noun's role in the sentence. These forms are called cases.

- The **subject** does the action. It is in the **nominative**, the basic form you find in a dictionary.
- The **direct object** is the person or thing the action is done to. It is in the **accusative** (Akkusativ).

To find the direct object, ask <De>Was?</De> (what?) for a thing or <De>Wen?</De> (whom?) for a person.

## Only masculine words change

<RuleTable equal>

| nominative | accusative |
| --- | --- |
| <De plain>der · ein</De> | <De plain>**den · einen**</De> |
| <De plain>die · eine</De> | <De plain>die · eine</De> |
| <De plain>das · ein</De> | <De plain>das · ein</De> |
| <De plain>die · –</De> | <De plain>die · –</De> |

</RuleTable>

Masculine words take <De>den</De> and <De>einen</De>. Feminine words (<De>die</De>), neuter words (<De>das</De>) and the plural look exactly as in the nominative. The plural has no <De>ein</De>: you use the noun on its own.

## The article shows who does what

Usually the subject comes first. But the direct object can also stand at the start of the sentence, for example to stress it. The verb stays in position 2. Then the article tells you who does what: <De>der</De> is the subject, <De>den</De> is the object. Both sentences below mean that the teacher asks the student.

**No accusative after <De>sein</De>.** <De>sein</De> (to be) has no direct object. The word after it stays in the nominative.

## Questions: <De>wer</De>, <De>wen</De>, <De>was</De>

The question word changes too, but only for people. <De>wer</De> asks for the person who does the action, <De>wen</De> for the person the action is done to. <De>was</De> asks for a thing and has the same form in both cases.

<RuleTable equal>

| subject (nominative) | object (accusative) |
| --- | --- |
| <De>wer?</De> for a person | <De>wen?</De> for a person |
| <De>was?</De> for a thing | <De>was?</De> for a thing |

</RuleTable>

## Verbs with a direct object

Many everyday verbs have a direct object in the accusative: <De>haben</De> (to have), <De>brauchen</De> (to need), <De>kaufen</De> (to buy), <De>möchten</De> (would like), <De>mögen</De> (to like), <De>sehen</De> (to see), <De>essen</De> (to eat), <De>trinken</De> (to drink), <De>lesen</De> (to read), <De>suchen</De> (to look for), <De>nehmen</De> (to take).

## <De>es gibt</De>: there is, there are

<De>es gibt</De> means "there is" or "there are". What comes after it is always in the accusative. <De>gibt</De> stays the same with the plural.

A few prepositions, such as <De>für</De> (for) and <De>ohne</De> (without), are also followed by the accusative.

## Pronouns in the accusative

Personal pronouns (<De>ich, du, er</De> …) have accusative forms too. <De>er</De> becomes <De>ihn</De>, just as <De>der</De> becomes <De>den</De>. Use <De>ihn</De> for masculine things as well, such as <De>der Schlüssel</De> (the key).

<RuleTable>

| singular | plural |
| --- | --- |
| <De plain>ich → **mich**</De> | <De plain>wir → **uns**</De> |
| <De plain>du → **dich**</De> | <De plain>ihr → **euch**</De> |
| <De plain>er → **ihn**, sie → sie, es → es</De> | <De plain>sie → sie, Sie → Sie</De> |

</RuleTable>

<AustrianNote id="at1">
  A few nouns have a different gender in Austria. <De>Joghurt</De> is usually neuter there (<De>das Joghurt</De>), while in Germany it is mostly masculine (<De>der Joghurt</De>). <De>Keks</De> (biscuit) is the same: <De>das Keks</De> in Austria. Only masculine words change in the accusative, so Austrians say <De>ein Joghurt</De> where most Germans say <De>einen Joghurt</De>. Both are correct standard German.
</AustrianNote>
