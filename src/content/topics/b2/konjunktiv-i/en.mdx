summary: To pass on what someone said without taking a side, written German uses the Konjunktiv I, as in *Anna sagt, sie habe keine Zeit*. Most forms are the stem + *-e*, like *habe, komme, müsse*, and *sein* becomes *sei*. When a form looks like the present tense, use the Konjunktiv II, like *sie hätten*. ## What the Konjunktiv I is for

The Konjunktiv I (subjunctive I) is a verb form for reported speech (indirekte Rede): you pass on what someone else said or thought. The form shows that these are the other person's words, and that you neither confirm nor deny them. You find it mostly in written German, for example in the news and in reports.

## How the forms are made

Take the infinitive without <De>-en</De>, the stem, and add the endings <De>-e, -est, -e, -en, -et, -en</De>. The stem keeps the vowel of the infinitive. Verbs that change their vowel in the present tense do not change it here: <De>er fährt</De> but <De>er fahre</De>, <De>er kann</De> but <De>er könne</De>.

The most important form is the one for <De>er</De>, <De>sie</De> and <De>es</De>: the stem + <De>-e</De>, as in <De>er habe</De>, <De>sie komme</De>, <De>es gebe</De>. It differs from the present tense (<De>er hat</De>, <De>sie kommt</De>, <De>es gibt</De>), so the reader sees at once that the words are reported.

## <De>sein</De>: <De>sei</De> and <De>seien</De>

<De>sein</De> does not follow the rule: the form for <De>ich</De> and <De>er / sie / es</De> is <De>sei</De>, without <De>-e</De>. All its forms differ from the present tense (<De>ich bin</De>, <De>wir sind</De>), so they always show that the words are reported.

## When the form looks like the present tense

For most verbs, the Konjunktiv I forms for <De>ich</De>, <De>wir</De> and <De>sie / Sie</De> are the same as the present tense: <De>wir haben</De>, <De>sie kommen</De>. Then nobody can see that the words are reported. In that case, use the Konjunktiv II (subjunctive II) instead. These are the forms you need most:

<RuleTable>

| verb | Konjunktiv II |
| --- | --- |
| <De plain>haben</De> | <De plain>ich **hätte**, sie **hätten**</De> |
| <De plain>können</De> | <De plain>ich **könnte**, sie **könnten**</De> |

</RuleTable>

For regular verbs like <De>spielen</De>, the Konjunktiv II looks like the Präteritum, the simple past (<De>sie spielten</De>). Then you can use <De>würde</De> + infinitive, as in the last example. The <De>würde</De> form is common in speech, but in writing many people avoid it.

Many people also use the Konjunktiv II when the Konjunktiv I would be clear, especially when they speak. To them, the Konjunktiv I sounds formal. So you will hear <De>er wüsste</De> instead of <De>er wisse</De>.

## The past and the future

**1. The past.** For something that happened before the person spoke, use <De>habe</De> or <De>sei</De> + the past participle (Partizip II). Choose <De>haben</De> or <De>sein</De> as in the Perfekt. With <De>ich</De>, <De>wir</De> and <De>sie / Sie</De>, <De>habe</De> becomes <De>hätte</De> or <De>hätten</De>, as above.

## With or without <De>dass</De>

Reported speech can follow the verb of saying directly. Then the verb stays in position 2, as in a normal statement. With <De>dass</De>, the verb goes to the end of the clause. Both are correct, and both take the Konjunktiv I.

## Questions and requests

**1. Questions.** A reported yes/no question starts with <De>ob</De> (whether). A reported question with a question word keeps that word, like <De>wann</De> or <De>wo</De>. In both, the verb goes to the end. A separable verb like <De>ankommen</De> stays in one piece there.
