---
title: 'Subordinate clauses with weil, dass, wenn'
summary: After *weil* (because), *dass* (that) and *wenn* (if, when), the verb goes to the end of the clause. A comma separates the clause from the rest of the sentence. When the clause comes first, the rest of the sentence starts with its verb.
---

## The verb at the end

A subordinate clause (Nebensatz) is a part of a sentence that cannot stand on its own. It starts with a word like <De>weil</De>, <De>dass</De> or <De>wenn</De>, and its verb, the form that changes with the person, goes to the very end. A comma always separates it from the main clause.

## <De>weil</De>: because

<De>weil</De> gives a reason. <De>denn</De> means the same, but after <De>denn</De> the verb stays in position 2, as in a normal sentence.

<RuleTable equal>

| <De>weil</De> | <De>denn</De> |
| --- | --- |
| verb at the end | verb in position 2 |

</RuleTable>

In everyday speech you will sometimes hear <De>weil</De> with the verb in position 2. This is not standard German, so in writing put the verb at the end.

## <De>dass</De>: that

<De>dass</De> often comes after verbs of saying, thinking and feeling, like <De>sagen</De>, <De>glauben</De>, <De>wissen</De> and <De>hoffen</De>. It joins what someone says or thinks to the rest of the sentence.

## <De>wenn</De>: if, when

**1. If.** <De>wenn</De> gives a condition: if one thing happens, the other one follows.

## Two verbs in the clause

**1. Modal verbs and the Perfekt.** The verb that changes with the person comes last, after the infinitive (like <De>schaffen</De>) or the past participle (like <De>gegangen</De>).

**2. Separable verbs.** At the end of the clause, the verb and its front part come together again and are written as one word: <De>ankommen</De> → <De>wenn du ankommst</De>.

## The clause first

The clause can also come first. Then the whole clause is position 1, so the rest of the sentence starts with its verb in position 2. Two verbs meet at the comma: verb, comma, verb.
