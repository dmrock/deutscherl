## How the Perfekt works

The Perfekt is the past tense people use most when they speak German. It has two parts:

1. a form of <De>haben</De> or <De>sein</De> in position 2 of the sentence,
2. the past participle (Partizip II, the form like <De>gesehen</De> or <De>gefahren</De>) at the very end.

## <De>haben</De> or <De>sein</De>?

Most verbs use <De>haben</De>. Use <De>sein</De> when the verb means one of these:

- **Movement to another place**: afterwards you are somewhere else (<De>gehen, fahren, fliegen, kommen</De>).
- **Change of state**: afterwards something is different (<De>aufwachen, aufstehen, einschlafen, werden</De>).

Two important verbs use <De>sein</De> even though nothing moves or changes: <De>sein</De> itself and <De>bleiben</De> (to stay).

<RuleTable>

| <De>haben</De> | <De>sein</De> |
| --- | --- |
| most verbs | movement to another place: <De>gehen, fahren, kommen</De> |
| verbs with an accusative object (the "what" of the action): <De>sehen, essen, machen</De> | change of state: <De>aufwachen, aufstehen, werden</De> |
| reflexive verbs (with <De>sich</De>): <De>sich freuen</De> | <De>sein</De> and <De>bleiben</De> |

</RuleTable>

**Tip:** ask yourself: after the action, is the person in another place or in another state? If yes, use <De>sein</De>. If not, use <De>haben</De>. Sleeping, for example, changes nothing, so it is <De>ich habe geschlafen</De>.

<AustrianNote id="at1">
  In Austria, Switzerland and southern Germany, the verbs <De>sitzen</De> (to sit), <De>liegen</De> (to lie) and <De>stehen</De> (to stand) usually form the Perfekt with <De>sein</De>. In northern and central Germany, people use <De>haben</De>. Both are correct standard German.
</AustrianNote>
