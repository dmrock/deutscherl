title: 'Adjective endings: der neue Tisch, ein neuer Tisch'
summary: An adjective before a noun gets an ending. After *der, die, das* it is *-e* or *-en*. After *ein, kein, mein* it is the same, except in three places where the adjective takes *-er* or *-es*, as in *ein neuer Tisch* and *ein neues Sofa*. After *sein* the adjective has no ending.

## An ending only before a noun

After <De>sein</De> (to be), an adjective keeps its basic form, the one in the dictionary. When the adjective stands between the article and the noun, it gets an ending. This change is called adjective declension (Adjektivdeklination).

The ending depends on three things: the gender of the noun, the case, and the article in front of the adjective. Look at the article first.

## After <De>der</De>, <De>die</De>, <De>das</De>

After the definite article there are only two endings, <De>-e</De> and <De>-en</De>.

<RuleTable wide>

| case | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nominative | <De plain>der neu**e** Tisch</De> | <De plain>die neu**e** Lampe</De> | <De plain>das neu**e** Sofa</De> | <De plain>die neu**en** Stühle</De> |
| accusative | <De plain>den neu**en** Tisch</De> | <De plain>die neu**e** Lampe</De> | <De plain>das neu**e** Sofa</De> | <De plain>die neu**en** Stühle</De> |
| dative | <De plain>dem neu**en** Tisch</De> | <De plain>der neu**en** Lampe</De> | <De plain>dem neu**en** Sofa</De> | <De plain>den neu**en** Stühle**n**</De> |

</RuleTable>

The ending is <De>-e</De> in five places: the nominative singular of all three genders, and the accusative with <De>die</De> and <De>das</De>. Everywhere else it is <De>-en</De>: after <De>den</De>, <De>dem</De>, the dative <De>der</De>, and in the plural.

## After <De>ein</De>, <De>kein</De>, <De>mein</De>

After <De>ein</De>, most endings are the same as after <De>der</De>. They differ in three places, where <De>ein</De> itself has no ending: the masculine nominative and the neuter nominative and accusative. There the adjective takes the ending that the definite article would show: <De>-er</De> as in <De>der</De>, <De>-es</De> as in <De>das</De>.

<RuleTable wide>

| case | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nominative | <De plain>ein neu**er** Tisch</De> | <De plain>eine neu**e** Lampe</De> | <De plain>ein neu**es** Sofa</De> | <De plain>keine neu**en** Stühle</De> |
| accusative | <De plain>einen neu**en** Tisch</De> | <De plain>eine neu**e** Lampe</De> | <De plain>ein neu**es** Sofa</De> | <De plain>keine neu**en** Stühle</De> |
| dative | <De plain>einem neu**en** Tisch</De> | <De plain>einer neu**en** Lampe</De> | <De plain>einem neu**en** Sofa</De> | <De plain>keinen neu**en** Stühle**n**</De> |

</RuleTable>

<De>ein</De> has no plural, so the table shows <De>kein</De> there. <De>kein</De> and the possessive articles (<De>mein, dein, sein, ihr, unser</De> …) take the same endings as <De>ein</De>, and in the plural the adjective always ends in <De>-en</De>. Without any article, the endings are different, for example <De>neue Stühle</De> (new chairs) in the nominative and accusative. This page covers adjectives after an article.

## Two shortcuts

**1. Dative: always <De>-en</De>.** After every dative article, the adjective ends in <De>-en</De>, with all genders and in the plural.

<Examples>
  <Example id="e17" />
  <Example id="e18" />
</Examples>

**2. Plural with an article: always <De>-en</De>.** After <De>die</De>, <De>keine</De>, <De>meine</De> and the other articles, a plural adjective ends in <De>-en</De> in every case: <De>die neuen Schuhe</De>, <De>keine freien Zimmer</De>, <De>mit meinen alten Freunden</De>.

**Tip:** learn adjectives in whole phrases, with an article and a noun, and in more than one case: <De>ein schöner Tag</De>, <De>einen schönen Tag</De>, <De>an einem schönen Tag</De>.

## Spelling changes

A few common adjectives change their spelling when they get an ending:

- <De>hoch</De> (high) loses the <De>c</De>: <De>der hohe Berg</De>, <De>ein hohes Haus</De>.
- <De>teuer</De> (expensive) loses the <De>e</De> before <De>r</De>: <De>ein teurer Urlaub</De>, <De>ein teures Hotel</De>.
- <De>dunkel</De> (dark) loses the <De>e</De> before <De>l</De>: <De>die dunkle Nacht</De>, <De>ein dunkles Kleid</De>.

## Words without an ending

Some words before a noun never get an ending:

- Adjectives made from city names with <De>-er</De>, like <De>Wiener</De> (from Vienna) or <De>Salzburger</De> (from Salzburg). They always start with a capital letter: <De>ein Wiener Schnitzel</De>, <De>die Salzburger Altstadt</De>.
- The colours <De>lila</De> (purple) and <De>rosa</De> (pink): <De>eine lila Tasche</De>, <De>ein rosa Hemd</De>. In casual speech you may hear forms like <De>eine lilane Tasche</De>, but they are not standard German.

<AustrianNote id="at1">
  In Austria, as in southern Germany and Switzerland, <De>Cola</De> is neuter: <De>das Cola</De>. In the rest of Germany you will also hear the feminine <De>die Cola</De>. The ending of the adjective follows the gender: Austrians say <De>ein kaltes Cola</De>, and with the feminine it is <De>eine kalte Cola</De>. Both are correct standard German.
</AustrianNote>
