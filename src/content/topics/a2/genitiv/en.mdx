summary: The genitive shows who something belongs to or what it is part of, like *das Handy meines Vaters* (my father's phone). The article changes to *des* or *der*, and masculine and neuter nouns add *-s* or *-es*. When people speak, they often use *von* + dative instead.

## What the genitive does

The genitive (Genitiv) is the case for "whose" and "of". It links two nouns: the second noun is the owner, or the thing the first noun belongs to. The genitive noun comes **after** the other noun, like "the phone of my father".

## Articles

The genitive changes the article, and for masculine and neuter nouns the noun too. <De>kein</De> works like <De>ein</De> (<De>keines</De>, <De>keiner</De>), and all possessives work like <De>mein</De>: <De>deines, seines, ihres, unseres</De> / <De>deiner, seiner, ihrer, unserer</De>.

Feminine and plural nouns never get an ending. Careful: the feminine genitive article <De>der</De> looks like the masculine <De>der</De>, but here it means "of the".

## <De>-s</De> or <De>-es</De>?

Masculine and neuter nouns add an ending:

- **-es** after a hissing sound (<De>s, ß, x, z, tz</De>): <De>des Hauses</De>, <De>des Arztes</De>, <De>des Platzes</De>.
- **-s** for most nouns with more than one syllable: <De>des Lehrers</De>, <De>des Autos</De>, <De>des Handys</De>.
- **Both** are correct for many short nouns of one syllable: <De>des Buches</De> or <De>des Buchs</De>, <De>des Tages</De> or <De>des Tags</De>.

A few masculine nouns end in <De>-n</De> instead (<De>des Kollegen</De>). You will learn them later.

## Names

With a name, add <De>-s</De> and put the name **before** the noun. The noun then has no article, and there is no apostrophe: <De>Annas Bruder</De>, not <De>Anna’s Bruder</De>. An apostrophe before the s appears only in shop names.

When the name ends in <De>s, ß, x, z</De> or <De>tz</De>, write only an apostrophe: <De>Hans’ Auto</De>, <De>Max’ Rucksack</De>. When speaking, many people avoid this and say <De>das Auto von Hans</De>.

## Set phrases of time and place

Some phrases with the genitive are very common: <De>am Anfang des Kurses</De> (at the beginning of the course), <De>am Ende des Monats</De> (at the end of the month), <De>in der Mitte der Stadt</De> (in the middle of the town), <De>in der Nähe des Bahnhofs</De> (near the station).

**Tip:** many compound nouns can be explained with the genitive: <De>die Haustür</De> is <De>die Tür des Hauses</De> (the door of the house).

## Genitive or <De>von</De> + dative?

When people speak, they often use <De>von</De> + dative instead of the genitive. <De>von dem</De> becomes <De>vom</De>. Both are correct standard German. The genitive sounds more formal and is typical of writing; <De>von</De> is relaxed and typical of speech.

Use <De>von</De> when a plural noun has no article: <De>der Preis von Äpfeln</De> (the price of apples).

Some prepositions also take the genitive, for example <De>wegen</De> (because of). They come later, at level B1.

<AustrianNote id="at1">
  In Austria, as in southern Germany, the genitive is rare in everyday speech. People say <De>von</De> + dative: <De>das Auto vom Papa</De>. In casual speech you will also hear the dative with <De>sein</De> or <De>ihr</De>, like <De>dem Papa sein Auto</De> (literally "to the dad his car"), and <De>wegen dem Regen</De> instead of <De>wegen des Regens</De>. <De>dem Papa sein Auto</De> is not standard German: understand it, but write the genitive or <De>von</De>.
</AustrianNote>
