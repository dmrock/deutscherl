## Three meanings

**1. Leave something somewhere.** You do not take it with you. Here <De>lassen</De> is the only verb.

## Forms

<RuleTable>

| | <De>lassen</De> |
| --- | --- |
| <De>ich</De> | <De>lasse</De> |
| <De>du</De> | <De>lässt</De> |
| <De>er / sie / es</De> | <De>lässt</De> |
| <De>wir</De> | <De>lassen</De> |
| <De>ihr</De> | <De>lasst</De> |
| <De>sie / Sie</De> | <De>lassen</De> |

</RuleTable>

With <De>du</De> and <De>er / sie / es</De>, the vowel changes from a to ä, like <De>fahren</De> → <De>du fährst</De>. With <De>ihr</De> there is no ä: <De>ihr lasst</De>.

To tell someone to do something: <De>Lass!</De> (to a person you call <De>du</De>), <De>Lasst!</De> (to <De>ihr</De>), <De>Lassen Sie!</De> (polite).

## Word order

<De>lassen</De> works like a modal verb (<De>können</De>, <De>müssen</De>): the form of <De>lassen</De> is in position 2, and the second verb is at the very end. When the sentence already has a modal verb, <De>lassen</De> itself goes to the end (see the dog example above).

## <De>lassen</De> in the Perfekt

The Perfekt of <De>lassen</De> uses <De>haben</De>. The last part depends on the meaning:

- **Without a second verb** (meaning 1), the past participle (Partizip II) is <De>gelassen</De>.
- **With a second verb** (meanings 2 and 3), there is no <De>gelassen</De>. Both verbs stand at the end, and <De>lassen</De> stays <De>lassen</De>.
