---
title: 'Adjectives without an article: frischer Kaffee, kalte Milch'
summary: When no article stands before the noun, the adjective shows the case itself. It takes the ending the definite article would have, as in *frischer Kaffee*, *mit kalter Milch* and *frisches Brot*. The same happens after numbers and words like *viele*, and in wishes like *Guten Tag*. Only the masculine and neuter genitive is different, as in *wegen schlechten Wetters*.
---

## When there is no article

Many nouns stand without an article: things you cannot count, like <De>Kaffee</De>, <De>Milch</De> or <De>Geld</De>, and plurals that mean "some" or things in general, like <De>Äpfel</De>. Then no article shows the case, so the adjective shows it. It takes the ending of the definite article: <De>der Kaffee</De> gives <De>frischer Kaffee</De>, <De>das Brot</De> gives <De>frisches Brot</De>, and <De>mit dem Brot</De> gives <De>mit frischem Brot</De>.

## The endings

<RuleTable wide>

| case | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nominative | <De plain>frisch**er** Kaffee</De> | <De plain>frisch**e** Milch</De> | <De plain>frisch**es** Brot</De> | <De plain>frisch**e** Äpfel</De> |
| accusative | <De plain>frisch**en** Kaffee</De> | <De plain>frisch**e** Milch</De> | <De plain>frisch**es** Brot</De> | <De plain>frisch**e** Äpfel</De> |
| dative | <De plain>frisch**em** Kaffee</De> | <De plain>frisch**er** Milch</De> | <De plain>frisch**em** Brot</De> | <De plain>frisch**en** Äpfel**n**</De> |

</RuleTable>

Compare each ending with the definite article: <De>-er</De> as in <De>der</De>, <De>-e</De> as in <De>die</De>, <De>-es</De> as in <De>das</De>, <De>-em</De> as in <De>dem</De>, <De>-en</De> as in <De>den</De>. So the masculine accusative ends in <De>-en</De>, the feminine dative in <De>-er</De>, and the dative plural in <De>-en</De>, with an <De>-n</De> on the noun as well.

## What is new compared with <De>ein</De>

In the singular nominative and accusative, the endings are the same as after <De>ein</De>: <De>ein frischer Kaffee</De> and <De>frischer Kaffee</De>, <De>einen frischen Kaffee</De> and <De>frischen Kaffee</De>, <De>ein frisches Brot</De> and <De>frisches Brot</De>. In the nominative, accusative and dative, only two places are new:

**1. The dative singular.** After <De>einem</De> and <De>einer</De> the adjective ends in <De>-en</De>, as in <De>an einem schönen Tag</De>. Without an article it ends in <De>-em</De> (masculine and neuter) or <De>-er</De> (feminine).

**2. The plural nominative and accusative.** After <De>keine</De> or <De>meine</De> the adjective ends in <De>-en</De>. Without an article it ends in <De>-e</De>: <De>frische Äpfel</De>, <De>alte Freunde</De>.

<AustrianNote id="at1">
  In Austria, whipped cream is <De>das Schlagobers</De>. In Germany it is <De>die Schlagsahne</De>. Because the gender is different, the dative ending is different too: <De>mit frischem Schlagobers</De>, but <De>mit frischer Schlagsahne</De>.
</AustrianNote>

## The genitive

You will meet the genitive (Genitiv) without an article after prepositions like <De>wegen</De> (because of) and <De>trotz</De> (despite).

- Feminine and plural nouns follow the rule: the adjective ends in <De>-er</De>, as in <De>der</De>. For example, <De>wegen großer Nachfrage</De>.
- Masculine and neuter nouns are the exception. The noun gets <De>-s</De> or <De>-es</De>, and the adjective ends in <De>-en</De>, not <De>-es</De>: <De>wegen schlechten Wetters</De>, <De>trotz starken Regens</De>.

## After numbers and <De>viele</De>, <De>einige</De>, <De>mehrere</De>

After numbers from <De>zwei</De> up, and after <De>viele</De> (many), <De>einige</De> (some, a few) and <De>mehrere</De> (several), the adjective takes the same endings as without an article. In the nominative and accusative it ends in <De>-e</De>, in the dative in <De>-en</De>: <De>zwei kleine Kinder</De>, <De>mit zwei kleinen Kindern</De>.

<De>alle</De> (all) is different. It works like <De>die</De>, so the adjective ends in <De>-en</De>: <De>alle neuen Kollegen</De>.

## Greetings and wishes

Many greetings and wishes are short forms of a sentence. You wish someone <De>einen guten Morgen</De> (<De>wünschen</De>, to wish), or you send someone <De>liebe Grüße</De> (<De>senden</De>, to send). The greeting is the object of the verb, so it is in the accusative. The short form leaves out the verb and the article:

- masculine, <De>-en</De>: <De>Guten Morgen</De>, <De>Guten Tag</De>, <De>Guten Abend</De>, <De>Guten Appetit</De>, <De>Herzlichen Glückwunsch</De>
- feminine, <De>-e</De>: <De>Gute Nacht</De>
- plural, <De>-e</De>: <De>Viele Grüße</De>, <De>Liebe Grüße</De>, <De>Frohe Weihnachten</De> (in wishes, <De>Weihnachten</De> is plural)

The letter ending <De>Mit freundlichen Grüßen</De> (kind regards) is in the dative after <De>mit</De>, so the plural ending is <De>-en</De>.

When you say goodbye, you can wish someone <De>einen schönen Tag noch</De> or just <De>schönen Tag noch</De> (have a nice day). The same goes for <De>(einen) schönen Abend noch</De>. The ending is <De>-en</De> either way, because after <De>einen</De> the masculine accusative also takes <De>-en</De>.
