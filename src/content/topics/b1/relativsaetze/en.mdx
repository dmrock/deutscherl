The pronoun takes its gender and number from the noun and its case from its role in the clause. Most forms are the same as the definite article. The new one is the dative plural *denen*.

## What a relative clause does

A relative clause (Relativsatz) gives more information about a noun. In English it starts with "who", "which" or "that". In German it starts with a relative pronoun (Relativpronomen): <De>der</De>, <De>die</De> or <De>das</De>.

A relative clause is a subordinate clause, like a clause with <De>weil</De> or <De>dass</De>. Its verb, the form that changes with the person, goes to the very end. A comma always separates it from the rest of the sentence.

## The relative pronouns

<RuleTable wide>

| case | masculine | feminine | neuter | plural |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| nominative | <De plain>der</De> | <De plain>die</De> | <De plain>das</De> | <De plain>die</De> |
| accusative | <De plain>den</De> | <De plain>die</De> | <De plain>das</De> | <De plain>die</De> |
| dative | <De plain>dem</De> | <De plain>der</De> | <De plain>dem</De> | <De plain>**denen**</De> |

</RuleTable>

In these three cases, the relative pronouns look like the definite article you already know. There is one exception: in the dative plural, the relative pronoun is <De>denen</De>, not <De>den</De>.

## Gender from the noun, case from the clause

You choose the pronoun in two steps. First, look at the noun the clause describes: its gender and number decide between masculine, feminine, neuter and plural. Then look at the relative clause itself: the pronoun's role in that clause decides the case. The case of the noun in the main clause does not matter.

**1. Nominative.** The pronoun is the subject of the relative clause: it does the action. In the first example, <De>einen Kollegen</De> is in the accusative, but the colleague is the one who speaks, so the pronoun is <De>der</De>.

## With a preposition

When the verb or phrase in the relative clause needs a preposition, like <De>mit</De>, <De>in</De>, <De>für</De> or <De>von</De>, the preposition comes first, right before the pronoun. The preposition decides the case, just as it does elsewhere: <De>mit</De> and <De>von</De> take the dative, <De>für</De> takes the accusative, and <De>in</De> takes the dative for a place.

## Where the clause goes

**1. In the middle of the sentence.** The relative clause usually comes right after the noun it describes. If the main clause goes on after it, the relative clause has a comma on both sides. Then two verbs meet at the second comma: the verb at the end of the relative clause, then the verb of the main clause.
