summary: The ending of a noun or the group it belongs to often shows its gender. Endings like *-ung* give *die*, *-chen* gives *das*, and days, months and seasons take *der*. The tables list the clues with examples and the main exceptions.

## How to use the clues

Most German nouns give no clue: you learn them with their article (<De>der Tisch</De>, <De>die Lampe</De>, <De>das Buch</De>). For many others, the ending or the meaning shows the gender. The clues on this page are true for most nouns of each kind, not for all of them, so the main exceptions are listed too.

Two rules help with every noun:

- In the plural, every noun takes <De>die</De>.
- A compound noun (two or more words joined into one) takes the gender of its last part: <De>der Bus</De> + <De>die Haltestelle</De> → <De>die Bushaltestelle</De>.

The endings <De>-ung</De>, <De>-heit</De>, <De>-keit</De>, <De>-schaft</De>, <De>-ion</De> and <De>-tät</De> are the safest: nouns with them are almost always feminine. <De>-in</De> marks women: <De>der Lehrer</De> → <De>die Lehrerin</De>.

There are a few exceptions with the other endings: <De>das Knie</De> (knee), <De>das Abitur</De> (the final school exam in Germany; in Austria it is <De>die Matura</De>).

Most nouns ending in <De>-e</De> are feminine too, but there are important exceptions:

- many nouns for male people, and some animals: <De>der Junge</De> (boy), <De>der Kollege</De> (colleague), <De>der Löwe</De> (lion);
- a few other nouns: <De>der Name</De>, <De>der Käse</De> (cheese), <De>das Auge</De> (eye), <De>das Ende</De> (end).

## Usually neuter: das

<De>-chen</De> makes a word small, and it always gives <De>das</De>, whatever the gender of the base word: <De>die Katze</De> → <De>das Kätzchen</De>. That is why <De>das Mädchen</De> (girl) is neuter. The ending <De>-lein</De> works the same way, but it is rare today and sounds old-fashioned or poetic.

Exceptions to the endings: <De>der Moment</De>, <De>die Firma</De> (company).

<De>Ge-</De> gives <De>das</De> when the noun names a group of things: <De>das Gebirge</De> (mountains, a mountain range), <De>das Gemüse</De> (vegetables). Other nouns with <De>Ge-</De> can have any gender: <De>der Geschmack</De> (taste), <De>die Gefahr</De> (danger), <De>der Geburtstag</De> (birthday), <De>die Geschichte</De> (story, history).

Verbs used as nouns are always neuter: <De>essen</De> → <De>das Essen</De> (the food, the meal), <De>schwimmen</De> → <De>das Schwimmen</De>.

<AustrianNote id="at2">
In Austria, many everyday words end in <De>-erl</De>, a small-word ending like <De>-chen</De>. These words are neuter too: <De>das Sackerl</De> (bag), <De>das Pickerl</De> (sticker), <De>das Kipferl</De> (crescent-shaped pastry). <De>Sackerl</De> and <De>Pickerl</De> are informal.
</AustrianNote>

## Usually masculine: der

<GermanTable id="masculine">Endings of masculine nouns</GermanTable>

<De>-er</De> gives <De>der</De> when the noun comes from a verb and names a person or a device: <De>lehren</De> (to teach) → <De>der Lehrer</De>, <De>wecken</De> (to wake up) → <De>der Wecker</De> (alarm clock). Other nouns ending in <De>-er</De> can have any gender: <De>die Mutter</De>, <De>das Fenster</De> (window).

<De>-or</De> gives <De>der</De> mainly in words from Latin for people and machines: <De>der Motor</De>, <De>der Doktor</De>. Other nouns ending in <De>-or</De> can differ: <De>das Tor</De> (gate, goal), <De>das Labor</De> (laboratory).

<De>-ent</De> for people is masculine, but <De>-ment</De> is neuter: <De>der Student</De>, <De>das Dokument</De>.

Some groups of nouns are masculine:

- days, months and seasons: <De>der Montag</De>, <De>der Mai</De>, <De>der Winter</De>;
- times of day: <De>der Morgen</De>, <De>der Mittag</De>, <De>der Abend</De>, but <De>die Nacht</De> (night);
- rain, snow and wind: <De>der Regen</De>, <De>der Schnee</De>, <De>der Wind</De>;
- male people: <De>der Mann</De>, <De>der Vater</De>, <De>der Bruder</De> (brother).
